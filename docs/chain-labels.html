Whose chain is whose — subtle options

Two chains on one stage. Charles has seven wheels, Harper has one. Every option below is drawn at honest proportions — module 7, teeth 13–19, the same engraving band the page uses. None of them adds text furniture to the stage unless it says so.

A — Per-person colour family (this is issue #68)

Each chain draws from its own region of the hue wheel. No new element type, nothing added to the stage, and it stays entirely inside the machine's existing language. Distinguishes chains instantly; says who only via the engraved handles.

B — A different centre family per person

The page already deals five centre families: holes, spokes, hex core, planetary, ravigneaux. Bias each person toward one. Extremely idiomatic — it looks like two batches of parts from different machines — but it competes with the planetary showpiece, which is dealt once per load on merit.

C — An owner's stamp in the engraving band

The band already carries a build marking on some wheels (M7 Z18 · 17-4PH DMLS). Add the owner as part of that stamp, so it reads as a manufacturing mark rather than a caption. Subtlest of the lot, and legible only close up — which may be exactly right, or may be too quiet.

D — A monogram on the hub cap of the lead wheel only

One wheel per chain carries an initial where a service icon would sit. Reads as the drive gear being marked. Cheap and unambiguous — but it consumes a hub cap, and every hub cap currently carries a service icon that is also a link.

E — Nothing at all (the current design)

The bands already carry each person's handles — /csmarshall, charles@wozi.com, harper@wozi.com. Identity is present for anyone who looks, and the machine is not captioned. Shown here so the others have something honest to be compared against.

F — Colour family plus the owner's stamp (A + C)

Distinguishable at a glance from the palette, identifiable close up from the stamp. Two quiet signals rather than one loud one. Costs both changes, but the stamp is a string change inside an engraving that already exists.

G1 — A scribed datum line behind each chain

The reference a fitter follows along a real machine: a scribed line with tick marks, running past both ends the way a datum does past the parts it references. Sits in the background layer with the ghost machinery, so it reads as part of the frame rather than as annotation. Identity comes from the stamped plate at the leading end.

G2 — A ground track the chain runs along

Same idea, heavier: a milled band the wheels sit on, cross-hatched the way a ground surface is. Stronger separation between chains — you can see at a glance that these are two assemblies — but it puts a lot more ink on the stage, and the page currently has nothing behind the wheels except soft ghosts and one shadow layer. Risk: reads as a UI panel rather than as machining.

G3 — Datum line, plate only, no colour split (recommended pairing)

Both chains keep the ordinary random palette — no hue is reserved to anyone — and identity lives entirely in the machined reference. This is the version that survives the colour requirement below: because a person's hue preference must not remove that hue from anyone else's deal, two chains can legitimately come out looking alike, so colour cannot be the thing that tells them apart. The datum can.

H — The chosen treatment, LIGHT theme

Datum drawn from the page's own tokens rather than an invented grey: --muted #6B7E7C for the scribe and plate text, --hair #D5DAD6 for the plate body, at the opacity the ghost layer already uses in light (0.46). The plate string comes from config, not from code.

H — The same treatment, DARK theme

Same construction, dark tokens: --muted #93A5A3 scribe, --hair #373E3D plate, at the ghost layer's dark opacity (0.17). Wheels are lighter in dark mode, as the page's tone derivation already makes them. This is the case to judge — a datum that works in light can vanish or shout in dark.

Note the opacity is 0.34 here, not the ghost layer's 0.17. At 0.17 a one-pixel scribe on a dark ground effectively disappears — a filled ghost wheel survives dimming that a hairline does not. If this treatment ships, that difference is a real value to derive rather than guess, and it should be measured against the rendered page rather than this mock.

I — Ticks aligned to the wheel centres (a station index)

Every major tick is a wheel, projected onto the datum and numbered; minor ticks subdivide the gap between stations. The line now means something — it is the index a fitter reads off to find part 03 — and it is derived from the solve, so it cannot drift out of step with the wheels it references. Compare against the arbitrary ruler in H above.

J — As specified: full breadth, plate at the last leading ghost (this is the one)

The datum spans the whole assembly including the background machinery and runs off the page on the trailing side. Stations mark only the clickable gears — ghosts are spanned but not indexed, because they are not parts you can reach. The plate sits alongside the last leading ghost, reading as the placard at the head of the run. Harper's single-gear chain gets the same treatment, so a short chain no longer looks stubby.
